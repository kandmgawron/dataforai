Embeddings on AWS

Why meaning matters more
than matching.

From RAG Prototype to Production: AI Data Architecture on AWS
In Video 2 you saw search fail. You searched for 'backpack for a week in Scotland' and got nothing. You tried the colour filter with 'red' and missed products tagged 'fiery_red'. This video explains why that happens and introduces the technology that fixes it: embeddings.
Why search fails

Tokens don't understand meaning

Failed searches from Video 2
  • "backpack for Scotland" → 1000s results, none really correct
  • Colour filter "red" → misses fiery_red
  • "waterproof winter gloves" → misses products described differently
How BM25 / keyword search works
  • Tokenises text into individual terms
  • Matches exact tokens only
  • No understanding of synonyms or intent
  • Ranks by term frequency, not meaning
Every failed search in Video 2 happened because keyword matching can't bridge the gap between what customers say and how products are described.
BM25 is the algorithm behind keyword search. It's been the standard for decades. It works by breaking text into tokens and matching them. If your query contains 'red' and the product data says 'fiery_red', there's no match. If you search 'backpack for Scotland', it looks for those exact words in product names and descriptions. If the product says 'multi-day hiking rucksack suitable for Scottish Highlands', BM25 doesn't know those are related. This isn't a bug. BM25 is doing exactly what it's designed to do: match tokens. The problem is that customers don't think in tokens. They think in meaning.
The solution

Text → numbers that capture meaning

How it works
  • Each word/phrase becomes a list of 1024 numbers
  • Similar meaning → nearby coordinates
  • "red" and "fiery_red" are close neighbours
  • "red" and "hiking" are far apart
What changes for search
  • Query: "backpack for Scotland"
  • Finds: products tagged hiking, mountaineering, waterproof
  • No exact token match needed
Embeddings convert text into a list of numbers — a vector — that captures the meaning of the text. The key insight: things with similar meanings get similar numbers. So 'red', 'crimson', and 'fiery_red' all end up as nearby points in this space. 'Hiking', 'Scotland', 'backpack', and 'mountaineering' cluster together because they're semantically related. When you search with embeddings, you're not matching tokens. You're asking 'find me the things closest in meaning to what I said'. That's why 'backpack for Scotland' can match a product tagged for hiking and mountaineering, even though it doesn't contain the word 'Scotland'. The numbers themselves are meaningless to humans — you can't look at [0.234, -0.891, 0.445, ...] and know what it means. But the distances between vectors are meaningful: closer = more semantically similar.
The trade-off

Choosing dimensions

What are dimensions?

Each dimension is one number in the vector. More dimensions = more nuance captured = better at distinguishing similar concepts.

"red jacket" → [0.234, -0.891, 0.445, ... × 1024]

Dimension options
  • 256 dims — fast, small storage, lower quality
  • 512 dims — balanced for most use cases
  • 1024 dims — highest quality, more storage, slower search
Decision factors
  • Catalogue size — 22k products? 1024 is fine. 10M+ products? Consider 256
  • Query precision — distinguishing "hiking boot" from "approach shoe" needs more dims
  • Storage cost — 4× more dims = 4× more bytes per product
  • Search latency — more dims = slightly slower nearest-neighbour search
For Meridian's 22k products, 1024 dimensions is the right choice. Storage is ~88 MB total. Search latency is under 50ms.
Dimensions are the number of values in each vector. Think of it like resolution — more dimensions means more detail captured about the meaning of the text. At 256 dimensions, you can tell 'jacket' from 'tent' but might struggle to distinguish 'waterproof hardshell' from 'softshell'. At 1024 dimensions, those subtle differences are captured. The trade-off is storage and speed. Each product needs 1024 floating point numbers stored (4 bytes each = ~4KB per product). For Meridian's 22,000 products, that's about 88 MB total — trivial. For a retailer with 10 million products, that's 40 GB, and you might choose 256 dims to keep costs down. For this course, we'll use 1024 throughout. It's the highest quality Titan offers and our catalogue is small enough that storage and search speed aren't concerns. If you're building for a much larger catalogue, 512 is usually the sweet spot of quality vs cost.

Three options on AWS for generating embeddings. Titan Embed v2 is the default choice for most workloads. It's Amazon's own model, available through Bedrock, and it's the cheapest option. 1024 dimensions gives you good quality without excessive storage. It supports 25 languages and handles English extremely well. Third-party models through Bedrock: Cohere Embed is the main alternative. It's stronger for multilingual workloads. There are also domain-specific options emerging. Higher cost than Titan. Open-source on SageMaker: for when you need full control. You can fine-tune on your own data, which can significantly improve quality for specialised domains. Cost-effective at massive scale because you're paying for compute, not per-token. But you take on the operational overhead of hosting. For Meridian, we'll use Titan Embed v2. It's the right choice for a UK retailer with 22,000 products in English. We'd reach for Cohere if Meridian expanded to Continental Europe, or SageMaker if they had millions of products and wanted domain-specific tuning.
